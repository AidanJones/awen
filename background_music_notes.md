# Background music while reading: notes for Awen

Sources checked 25 October 2026. These findings concern the studied tasks and participants; they do not establish that an adaptive soundtrack improves enjoyment, immersion or comprehension when reading novels.

## What the evidence supports

- **Lyrics are a clearer distraction risk than instrumental texture.** Souza and Barbosa (2023) compared silence, instrumental lo-fi and lyrical music across cognitive tasks. Lyrical music impaired several outcomes, including reading comprehension; instrumental music did not credibly improve or impair performance. Subjective impressions of benefit did not establish objective gains. [Original study, Journal of Cognition](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.273).
- **Intensity and tempo can interact.** Thompson, Schellenberg and Letnic (2012) tested combinations of fast/slow and loud/quiet instrumental music. The fast-and-loud condition fell below the silence baseline. This supports avoiding that combination; it does not prove that one slow tempo or one instrumental genre is optimal. [Authors' paper](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/E-Schellenberg/publication/230746037_Fast_and_loud_background_music_disrupts_reading_comprehension/links/09e4150e021dc0898c000000/Fast-and-loud-background-music-disrupts-reading-comprehension.pdf).
- **Broader findings require careful distinctions.** Vasilev, Kirkby and Angele's (2018) Bayesian meta-analysis covered 65 studies of auditory distraction. Aggregate music effects were small and negative, with lyrics and intelligible speech particularly disruptive. Their lyrical/nonlyrical comparison did not establish a detrimental instrumental-music effect. Do not translate the aggregate estimate into “all instrumental music harms reading.” [Original meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6139986/).
- **Preference is relevant but not a guaranteed performance boost.** Que and Hu (2025) tested 52 nonnative English speakers using their preferred background music. Overall comprehension accuracy and reading time were essentially unchanged between music and silence; associations with musical characteristics and individual differences were more complicated. Preferred music can be an experience choice without an established comprehension advantage. [Original study, Reading Research Quarterly](https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/rrq.70004).

## Design inference, not an experimental result

These papers do not establish that orchestral scoring is better than drones, that synthesised strings are better than sine tones, or that story-adaptive changes are beneficial. The first ensemble experiment used held harmony, which the user found too droney and annoying. The revised artistic option uses finite plucked/woodwind-like phrases, occasional short chords, brief bass notes and intentional rests. This change responds to user experience, not a new claim about cognitive performance. Avoid loud dramatic crescendos, vocals and constant musical events. Preserve fixed ambience and silence as comparison conditions, and keep the reader in control of volume and layers.

The ensemble in this MVP is procedural synthesis without samples, not a realistic orchestral renderer. Listening studies should measure immersion and distraction separately from comprehension, comparing silence, fixed ambience and adaptive scoring at comparable loudness. A pleasant or more interesting sound is not evidence of improved reading performance.
